What is AI?

Normal software
A person writes the rules, step by step. It only ever does what it was told.
AI
Nobody writes the rules. The machine learns the pattern from examples.

Machines that learn a task from data — instead of being told exactly how to do it.

Now — the skill itself

So what is prompt engineering?

Writing your request so the model gives you what you actually want — the first time.

It is not
Magic words. Secret phrases. Code.
It is
Saying clearly what a good colleague would need to do the job.

One minute. This is the sentence they'll repeat if someone asks them later what the session was about.

A fair question

So why learn this at all?

The most powerful models forgive a vague prompt. They'll often figure out what you meant anyway.

So why not just always use the biggest model?

Take a minute — shout out your answers.

Pose the question and stop. Let the room answer for a minute — someone will say "cost" or "speed," which is exactly where you're going. Then advance to reveal the two reasons.

Why not just use the biggest model

Two reasons

Speed
Lightweight models answer faster. In production, speed is what enterprises pay for.
Cost
You pay per token. The biggest model on every small task gets expensive fast.

A clear prompt lets a smaller, cheaper, faster model do the job. That's the skill.

Reveal after they've guessed. Speed and cost are the enterprise reasons. The punchline — clarity lets a cheaper model succeed — is the buy-in for the whole session.

The unit that costs money

Tokens: the model's brain cells

A token is a chunk of text — roughly ¾ of a word. The model reads tokens and writes tokens.

More tokens, better answers
More context and more reasoning usually means a sharper result.
More tokens, bigger bill
You pay by the token — and a bigger model spends more of them per task.

The game: a good answer without burning tokens. A clear prompt does exactly that.

Ties speed + cost + reasoning together. The pixel row is a stand-in for tokens. Keep it light — no tokenization internals, just "text costs money, clarity saves it."

The whole idea

You already know how to do this

Asking a person

Vague: "Can you handle the thing for Friday?"

→ "…which thing? what do you need from me?"

Clear: "Can you email the client the final invoice PDF before Friday 5pm, and CC me?"

→ Done. No back-and-forth.

Asking the AI

Vague: "Write something about our launch."

→ Generic filler. Wrong length, wrong reader.

Clear: "Write a 3-sentence launch note for our newsletter, friendly tone, focus on the new dashboard."

→ Usable on the first try.

Same skill. One difference: a person asks when they're unsure — the AI just guesses. So you say up front what a good colleague would have asked you.

The clearest proof of the thesis. Land it slowly. The vague/clear pairs are deliberately mundane so nobody feels lectured. The punchline — the AI can't ask you to clarify — is why prompting feels like a skill when it's really front-loaded clarity.

Communication example · 1 of 3

Working memory

A person
Holds only about 4–7 things in mind at once. Bury the key detail in paragraph six and it's gone.
The model
The context window is a hard limit too. Lead with what matters, and cut what it doesn't need.

Don't make it hold more than it can. Put the important part first.

Source: Miller, The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two (Psychological Review, 1956); revised to about 4 items — Cowan (2001). The model's version is the fixed-size context window.

One idea: capacity is limited, for people and for models. Same fix — lead with what matters, drop the noise.

Communication example · 2 of 3

Salience markers

Emphasis tells a listener what matters. Structure does the same for the model.

Bland — everything weighs the same
summarise the report and pull out the risks and keep it short and use plain language and don't include the appendix and send it to the finance lead
Marked up — the point jumps out
Task: Summarise the report for the finance lead.
Focus: the risks.
Rules: short · plain language · skip the appendix

Markdown

## Task Summarise for the finance lead. **Focus:** risks **Skip:** the appendix

XML tags

<task>Summarise for the finance lead</task> <focus>risks</focus> <skip>the appendix</skip>

Source: Anthropic's prompt-engineering docs recommend XML tags to structure prompts; Markdown headings and lists do the same job.

Show bland vs marked-up first — the room should FEEL the difference. Then the two ways to mark structure: Markdown (## / ** / -) and XML tags. Both tell the model where the important parts are.

Communication example · 3 of 3

The long meeting

In a two-hour meeting, the start and the end stick. The middle blurs.

Long contexts do the same: models are sharpest on what sits at the beginning or end, and miss what's buried in the middle.

So: fresh chat per task, and put the key info first or last.

Source: serial-position effect — Murdock (1962), J. Exp. Psychology (we recall beginnings and ends). In models: Liu et al. (2023), "Lost in the Middle" — a U-shaped accuracy curve across context position (arXiv:2307.03172).

The strongest parallel of the three: people and models both lose the middle. The U-curve is the shape both show. This is the honest source for what's loosely called "token / context degradation."

Same request, twice

Watch the difference

Prompt A

Summarise this customer feedback.

Bland, generic, and you still don't know what to do next.

Prompt B — the same ask, with the four questions answered

[WHO] You are a product analyst writing for the engineering lead. [WHAT] Pull the top 3 recurring issues from this feedback. [WITH WHAT] Use only the messages below. Ignore one-off gripes. [GOOD LOOKS LIKE] 3 bullets, one sentence each, most frequent first. No preamble.

Prompt B isn't longer for the sake of it. It just answers the four things you'd tell a competent stranger.

Run both live if you can, on the same messy input. Don't tidy A up in advance — let the room watch it underperform. This sets up the four buckets and Activity 1.

Anatomy · 0:17–0:26

Every good prompt answers four questions

RWHO
Role. Who should the AI be, and who is this for?
IWHAT
Instructions. What exactly is the task?
CWITH WHAT
Context. What does it need in the box?
EGOOD LOOKS LIKE
Expected output. Format, length, tone, one sample.

These four are just what you'd tell a competent stranger. The letters spell RICE.

Walk each card against Prompt B. The buckets are the plain-language version; RICE is the name to carry out the door. Don't drill three acronyms — see next slide.

The name to remember

RICE

RRole — who it should be · WHO
IInstructions — what to do · WHAT
CContext — the info it needs · WITH WHAT
EExpected output — what good looks like · GOOD LOOKS LIKE

CARE and TAPE reorder the same four. Learn one.

The take-home. If they remember one thing, it's RICE mapped onto plain questions. The cheatsheet uses RICE too, so it's consistent with what they'll hold.

Iterating · 0:59–1:08

Coach it like an employee

The AI just handed you a rough draft. You're the manager — how do you get a better one back?

The vague boss
"No. Do it again."
The employee doesn't know what was wrong — so you get the same draft back. Louder isn't clearer.
The good manager
  1. 1Name the problem. "Paragraph 2 invented a deadline that isn't in the data."
  2. 2Say what good looks like. "Use only dates that appear in the source."
  3. 3Ask for a fix, not a restart. "Redo that paragraph; keep the rest."
Fixing an AI draft is just coaching a teammate — specific, actionable, and you never make them start from zero.

The coaching frame: you're the manager, the draft is an employee's first attempt. Live demo — take the weakest Activity 1 output (ask permission) and coach it in two turns on screen. Then point at "consistent" on the next slide.

Before you trust any output

Three questions

Accurate?
Every fact and figure correct against what you know?
Complete?
Anything missing you'd have to add by hand?
Consistent?
Would it hold up on the next fifteen inputs — not just this one?
You can't answer the third one yet. You've run your prompt once. That's the next activity.

Activity 2 · 1:08–1:32

One prompt, fifteen rows

In a chat window, "reusable prompt" is just an idea. Here you watch it become real — and watch it break.

Fifteen support tickets. One prompt. Get a summary, a priority, and a next action for every one.

The notebook scores you. Goal: 15 / 15.

This is the payoff of the session. The dataset is 15 synthetic tickets across five teams. Seven are traps built to break a loose prompt (sparse, two issues, resolved, two questions, rambling, and a shouty low-impact one). The notebook grades every answer and prints a report — most people start near 0/15.

Activity 2 · pick your path

Two ways in, same lesson

Start here · Notebook
  1. 1Open the Colab link. In the first cell, pick Gemini or Groq and paste your key. Run Setup.
  2. 2Edit the prompt in step 2. Only that cell.
  3. 3Run one ticket, then all fifteen.
  4. 4Read your score. Fix the prompt, re-run. Aim for 15/15.
If that won't run · No code
  1. 1Open the ticket sheet (Excel or text).
  2. 2Write your prompt in your usual chat tool.
  3. 3Paste all fifteen tickets. Ask for a table.
  4. 4Scan it yourself for the odd ones out.

Rate limit on Gemini? Switch to Groq in the first cell and re-run Setup — it's faster anyway.

Most of the room codes, so the notebook is the default; no-code is a real fallback. One TA on triage (usually keys and rate limits — the fix is flipping PROVIDER to groq), the rest roaming for prompt help. At 19 min have anyone on the no-code path glance at a neighbour's score report — that's the Python pitch, made by a peer.

Scan all fifteen.

Find the ones that don't match the others.

That inconsistency was always in your prompt. You just couldn't see it when you ran it once.

Then read the report under the table. It tells you exactly what broke — and on which tickets.

Say this BEFORE they run step 4. With the vague seed prompt most people score near 0/15 — format, length and priority failures on almost every row, plus the traps. That low first score is the hook. Then four minutes to fix the prompt and re-run; watching the score climb is the reward.

Debrief · 1:32–1:40

Where did it break?

What was your first score — and your best?
Which failure showed up on the most tickets?
Which bucket was missing from your prompt?

Almost always GOOD LOOKS LIKE — unconstrained format is the #1 source of inconsistency at volume.

Let them arrive at it. The report groups failures by how many tickets they hit — format and length usually top the list, which is the GOOD LOOKS LIKE bucket. The trap failures (answering a question, trusting the shouty ticket) are the WITH WHAT bucket: rules the model can't guess. Ask who hit 15/15 and what their prompt said.
